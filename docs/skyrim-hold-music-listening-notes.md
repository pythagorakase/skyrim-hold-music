## How to Use This Guide

Start each hold with the two named anchor recordings, then sample the other tracks to determine which attributes persist across performances. When evaluating generated audio, first listen **without the hold label**, then ask whether its *musical structure* distinguishes it from its nearest neighbor. Assess the number of audible performers as carefully as melody, rhythm, and production quality.

**Phase 1 assumption:** One visible lute-family instrumentalist, either instrumental or singing while playing. Ensemble recordings illustrate musical ideas; they do **not** authorize invisible choirs, drums, additional strings, or simultaneous independently performed parts. Future phases could permit other visible instruments and accompanists.

### Shared Nord Baseline

- **Reference tradition:** Scandinavian medieval narrative ballad.
- **Anchor recordings:** Kirsten Bråten Berg — **“Bendik Og Årolilja”** and **“Heiemo Og Nykkjen.”**
- **Listen for:** Strophic storytelling, modal melodies, a recognizable short refrain, plain vocal articulation, and recurring phrases rather than a large modern verse/chorus build.
- **Target reduction:** Dorian or Mixolydian melody, tonic/fifth drone, simple plucked melody and sparse ornaments.
- **Do not copy literally:** Organ/harmonium accompaniment in these recordings, or any ensemble texture absent from the visible roster.

## Hold 1: The Reach — Markarth

**Reference:** Gaelic sean-nós singing (air branch); *puirt-à-beul* or mouth music (dance branch). These are **separate forms**, not two tempos imposed on the same generic accompaniment.

### Listening Cues

- **Airs:** Flexible, text-shaped rhythm; a melody that unfolds slowly; connected grace notes and more extended ornamentation; expressive pauses governed by breath rather than a strict measure.
- **Dances:** Fast-moving articulated syllables, melodic runs, and a continuously recognizable rhythmic pulse. Voice itself creates much of the dancing impetus.
- **Across both:** Extra ornament makes sense; automatic repetitive strumming does not. A singer can carry the main rhythmic or expressive identity with only spare lute support.

**First A/B:** Joe Heaney's **“Roisin Dubh”** versus John MacInnes & John MacLeod's **“Puirt-A-Beul (Mouth Music)”**. The contrast is breath-led phrasing versus pulse-led vocal articulation.  
**Second A/B:** Darach Ó Catháin's **“An Caisideach Bán”** versus Julie Fowlis's **“Puirt - À - Beul Set: Ribinnean Riomhach.”** See whether the same broad regional vocabulary survives very different phrasing and production.

**Reject or rework if:** The air is forced into a steady pop-like strum; the dance loses its pulse; every track becomes an undifferentiated “Celtic tavern” piece; an invisible ensemble replaces the single bard.

## Hold 2: Falkreath — Falkreath

**Reference:** Karelian and Ingrian lament traditions and Finnic runo-song. The target combines an expressive lament quality with the reiterated melodic cells of epic singing.

### Listening Cues

- **Lament branch:** Grief-shaped delivery, vocal inflection, restrained range, elastic phrasing; the emotional expression lives in small changes rather than a large melodic climax.
- **Runo-song branch:** Compact, repeatable melodic material carries new words and narrative content. Notice the repeated gesture rather than looking for changing chord sequences.
- **Project-specific reduction:** Slow tempo, range generally within a fifth, short descending cells, and **perceptible five-beat repetition**. This last feature is an authored Falkreath constraint—not a general claim about the historical meter of Karelian lament.

**First A/B:** Emmi Kuittinen's **“Kaiho”** versus Outi Pulkkinen's **“Hiien hirvi.”** Compare open, grief-led delivery with repeated narrative cells.  
**Second A/B:** **“Kurja kyynelikko”** versus **“Lemminkäisen virsi.”** Is the distinction still audible across longer pieces?

**Reject or rework if:** The generator produces only a generic sad melody; the five-beat grouping disappears; melodic range spreads too far; it substitutes a sustained, elaborate Dunmer-style melisma or a plain Breton-style text narrative. Some recordings feature ensembles or bowed lyre: their instruments are **not** a target for the current lute animation.

## Hold 3: Hjaalmarch — Morthal

**Reference:** Lithuanian *sutartinės*—historically multipart songs marked by interlocking repetition and prominent adjacent-pitch dissonance. This is the **musical inspiration**, not a request for an unseen polyphonic choir.

### Listening Cues

- **Primary acoustic phenomenon:** Two nearby pitches can rub against one another rather than resolving into stable triads. Staggered entries and brief cells create friction through repetition.
- **Hjaalmarch translation:** Slow, hushed, reiterated phrases; neighboring-note turns; clashing seconds, including lute figures shadowed roughly a whole step apart; unstable, lingering endings.
- **Single-bard constraint:** A solo lute can alternate or imply interacting lines; a singer and lute could place neighboring tones against one another. With a lone flute, only sequential, monophonic turns are possible. Do not prompt for literal ensemble *sutartinės* unless performers exist on screen.

**First A/B:** Trys Keturiose's **“Bite, dabiłėli, dabilio”** against **“Bitela bičiuke, dobilio.”** Listen for independent attacks and moments of second-interval friction.  
**Secondary contrast:** **“Bite, dabiłėli, dabilio”** against Elektroninės Sutartinės's **“Du Dobilėliu.”** Does the cell structure remain detectable after a wholesale production change?

**Reject or rework if:** The music is merely creepy and minor-key; there is no neighboring-tone interaction; a bright, continuous Argonian-style cyclic pattern replaces the slow friction; or an actual multi-singer arrangement appears with only one bard present.

## Hold 4: Winterhold — Winterhold

**Reference:** Sámi joik, particularly exposed short figures and individual variation. The intended identity is **circularity**, not stereotypical “northern shamanic” atmosphere.

### Listening Cues

- Short melodic figures return with small changes in pitch, articulation, and vocables; a conventional verse/chorus architecture is not required.
- A phrase can feel as though it could continue to circle, without an emphatic terminal cadence.
- For this regional treatment: sparse voicing, intimacy, and space between returning figures. Traditional joik itself is not necessarily slow, mournful, or unaccompanied.
- The sound of a single singer is especially important for current implementation. Any modern rhythm bed is a production comparison, not an arrangement requirement.

**First A/B:** Wimme Saari's **“Rieban (The Fox)”** and **“Boazu (The Reindeer).”** Listen for recurring identity in very short performances.  
**Second A/B:** Johan Eira's **“Ole”** and Elen-Anne Buljo's **“Lemet-Anna.”** Compare differences of vocal timbre and phrasing without losing joik's identifiable figures.

**Reject or rework if:** The generator imposes grand cinematic build-ups, a verse/chorus pop song, or a strongly final cadence; produces unchanging drone without recognizable figures; or makes Winterhold indistinguishable from the Pale's **long complete calls**.

## Hold 5: Haafingar — Solitude

**Reference:** The Danish court of Christian IV, especially John Dowland's lute dances and airs. This is the most literally compatible profile with the one-lute prototype.

### Listening Cues

- **Galliards:** Clear Renaissance triple or six-pulse dance framework; ornaments and passagework preserve the underlying motion.
- **Contrasting courtly forms:** Almains introduce duple dance; a pavan introduces spacious, stately phrases; a fancy displays freer contrapuntal thought. These offer range within one courtly repertoire rather than making all tracks galliards.
- **Sung airs:** Intimate, shaped phrases with deliberate cadences and a supportive plucked accompaniment.
- **Haafingar target:** Polished and refined; recognizable triple dance where appropriate; spacious instrumental answers; elegant, clear returns to cadence notes.

**First A/B:** Nigel North's **“The King of Denmark's Galliard”** and **“The Frog Galliard.”** Compare how much decoration can coexist with a secure dance pulse.  
**Second A/B:** Paul O'Dette's **“A Dream (Lady Leighton's Paven)”** and **“Mrs. Winters Jumpp.”** Both are plausibly played by one lutenist but project very different characters.

**Reject or rework if:** The playing sounds like technical display without rhythmic identity; the meter morphs into Whiterun's irregular folk sway or the Rift's jig bounce; the arrangement adds a separate singer/instrumentalist when the actual roster contains one bard. The voice-plus-lute recordings use two real-world musicians, but their *relationship* can be adapted to one singing player.

## Hold 6: Eastmarch — Windhelm

**Reference:** Icelandic *rímur*, chanted narrative poetry. Unlike Winterhold joik, Eastmarch's material has **direction through articulated words and story**.

### Listening Cues

- Syllabic, speech-shaped epic phrases; narrow pitch range and exposed melody.
- Poetic organization supplies real form even where rhythm sounds speech-like or relatively free. “Improvised muttering” is not a good substitute.
- The lute should shadow the tune sparsely with isolated notes and bare fifths between vocal phrases. Melodic storytelling takes precedence over a recurring harmonic progression.
- Traditional *tvísöngur* is multipart, but the Phase 1 Eastmarch reference is **rímur only**; avoid generating an extra voice.

**First A/B:** Steindór Andersen and Jón Lárusson, both performing **“Rímur af Göngu-Hrólfi.”** Compare two singers' articulation and phrasing in versions of the same named narrative.  
**Second A/B:** A short Steindór solo excerpt against one of the *Raddir / Voices* archival singers; then hear **“Fjöll Í Austri”** only as a modern treatment, not the target band arrangement.

**Reject or rework if:** The piece becomes a solemn but ordinary pop-format song; exposed storytelling disappears beneath accompaniment; nonsensical murmuring replaces articulated verse; short circular vocables make it indistinguishable from Winterhold.

## Hold 7: The Rift — Riften

**Reference:** Playford-era English country dances and broadside ballads. A particularly suitable register for jokes, scandals, tavern stories, and current-event songs.

### Listening Cues

- **Dance branch:** Bounce and forward momentum, especially recognizable 6/8 jigs, with short memorable phrases.
- **Ballad branch:** Energetic, readily repeatable strophes, simple tuneful returns and refrains; witty or bawdy words can carry the performance without changing the underlying tune every verse.
- **Tonal and delivery choices:** Major/Mixolydian color, crisp lute attacks and syllabic, direct vocal delivery.
- Not **every** Playford dance is in 6/8. The two explicitly titled jigs are the stronger rhythmic references; other country dances show the broader repertoire.

**First A/B:** The Broadside Band's **“The Geud Man of Ballangigh; to a New Scotch Jigg”** against **“The Fits Come on Me Now or the Bishop of Chester's Jigg.”** Listen for the compound-meter bounce.  
**Second A/B:** Baltimore Consort's **“Watkin's Ale”** versus City Waites & Lucy Skeaping's **“The Jovial Broom Man.”** Listen for communal singability, comic character, and repeated tune structure.

**Reject or rework if:** The generator offers only generic cheerful medieval music; the bouncing 6/8 turns into a courtly galliard; every verse introduces unrelated new melody; an imaginary crowd or full Renaissance band appears in a one-bard scene. Audience refrains become feasible only when visible participants are supported.

## Hold 8: The Pale — Dawnstar (Original Palette)

**Status:** Superseded as the active target on 2026-10-13 by the iorram baseline. This retains the original composite coastal-air research, not one real-world tradition. Its inputs are Scottish/Shetland slow airs and Hebridean Gaelic vocal phrasing.

### Listening Cues

- Long, exposed melodic calls traverse a broader range than Falkreath's narrow cells.
- Slow rocking **compound** pulse in the target; widely spaced low lute notes mark that pulse.
- Phrases arrive at *distinct endings*, followed by audible empty space. The next call is a new, complete statement—not another iteration of a tiny loop.
- Solo vocal phrasing and instrumental airs offer complementary examples, not a fixed historical genre.
- **Metric caution:** “Hector the Hero” is a 6/8 example; “Da Slockit Light” is in 4/4 and supplies phrase-shape and emotional-space evidence, **not** the proposed meter.

**First A/B:** Aly Bain & Phil Cunningham's **“Hector the Hero”** versus **“Sitting in the Stern of a Boat.”** Focus on arcs, arrivals and pace.  
**Second A/B:** **“An Eala Bhán”** (the *Dual* recording) versus Julie Fowlis's **“Tha Mo Ghaol Air Àird a' Chuain.”** Compare broad sung lines and breathing between thoughts.  
**Useful same-song comparison:** The two versions of **“An Eala Bhán”** (tracks 9 and 15).

**Reject or rework if:** The result relies on vague sea-wind atmospherics rather than a coherent melody; every gap is filled by pads/arpeggios; or the long calls collapse into short joik-like circles. **Difficulty identified:** The explicit silence-and-complete-call instruction may be hard for a generator to obey consistently.

## The Pale — Alternate Palette: Hebridean *Iorram*

**Status:** Adopted as the provisional Pale baseline on 2026-10-13; listening tests remain pending. An *iorram* is a Scottish Gaelic rowing song. Compared with the original mixed slow-air palette, this supplies a more concrete musical organizing principle: **a recurring imagined oar-stroke pulse beneath strophic narrative melody**.

### Listening Cues

- Repeated strokes or a gently rocking rhythmic cycle provide coherence, rather than the instruction to leave conspicuous silence after every phrase.
- Long, singable lines and a recurrent verse or refrain may unfold over the rowing pulse. Short breaths and natural line endings suffice; rigid silence is optional.
- For the Dawnstar adaptation, **slow 6/8 is a deliberately selected possibility, not the universal meter of historical rowing songs**. Original songs could be communal, quicker, responsorial or rhythmically diverse.
- A single lute can mark the low oar-stroke pulse and provide spare melodic punctuation; a single bard can supply the text-bearing melody.
- Beware overlap with the Reach's Gaelic references: the Pale should be *measured and work-rhythmic*, not a free ornamented sean-nós air or fast mouth-music dance.

**First A/B:** Màiri Cheocaidh's **“An t-Iorram Niseach”** versus Fiona MacKenzie's **“Iorram Suirghe.”** Focus on how rhythm and song text fit one another.  
**Instrumental A/B:** Ossian's **“Iomramh Eadar Il' A's Uist”** versus Lauren MacColl's **“Iorram Iomraimh.”** Is motion perceptible without a sung text?  
**Historical/modern A/B:** **“An t-Iorram Niseach”** versus **“AN_”** by Adam, Mairearad Green & Mike Vass. The latter is an interpretive comparison, not an authenticity benchmark.

**Important classification caveat:** Not every recording below is a pure archival *iorram*. Tracks include a waulking-song set, later song adaptations, neighboring maritime music, fantasy-game scoring, and a documentary soundtrack. They help identify possible musical features without collapsing those separate sources into one tradition.

## Hold 9: Whiterun — Whiterun

**Reference:** Swedish and Danish narrative ballads and Scandinavian *polska*. This is the everyday Nord regional baseline, not a more austere, aristocratic or bizarre special case.

### Listening Cues

- **Ballads:** Strophic, Dorian/Mixolydian, a short returning refrain, clear syllables and a melody plain enough to remember. Ringing tonic-and-fifth drones provide grounding.
- **Polska:** Broad three-beat sway, often with **unequal beat lengths** or elastic emphasis rather than a uniformly spaced waltz pulse. A polska is emphatically **not** a Central European polka.
- **Common identity:** Both song and dance should share an earthy modal idiom, even when their tempos diverge. One singing bard can tell many stanzas with one recurring tune.
- **Lute reduction:** Open-string drones, simple upper melody, and rhythmic placement that suggests traditional fiddle-dance weight without copying fiddle or ensemble sound sources.

**Ballad A/B:** Ale Möller & Lena Willemark's **“Mannelig”** versus Triakel's **“Oväntad Bröllopsgäst.”** Listen for self-contained, repeatable narrative tunes across contrasting accompaniment styles.  
**Polska A/B:** **“Polska Efter Roligs Per”** versus Frifot's **“Polska Efter Ollas Per.”** Listen for triple sway and asymmetric beat emphasis.  
**Boundary A/B:** Any Whiterun polska versus a Haafingar galliard and a Rift jig. All are danceable; their metrical character should still be separable.

**Reject or rework if:** Every polska turns into a mechanical waltz or fast polka; the drone vanishes into generic major/minor pop chords; ballads have giant modern choruses; the overall timbre is too courtly (Haafingar) or rowdy (the Rift).

## Cross-Hold Blind-Listening Challenges

These are the most revealing adjacent-palette tests. A convincing recording must convey its hold **without** depending on its title, regional label or production polish.

| Comparison | What Must Differ |
|---|---|
| **Winterhold vs. the Pale (Original)** | Short *circular* vocables and weak cadence vs. long *complete* calls with space afterward |
| **Winterhold vs. the Pale (*Iorram*)** | Unmetered/loosely metered recurrence vs. recurring rowing-stroke pulse and strophic singing |
| **The Reach vs. the Pale (*Iorram*)** | Highly ornamented free air or fast mouth music vs. measured maritime work-song pulse |
| **Falkreath vs. Dunmer vs. Breton Folk** | Short, narrow, descending five-beat cells vs. long modal melisma vs. plain text-led stanzas |
| **Hjaalmarch vs. Argonian** | Slow neighboring-tone friction and unresolved releases vs. continuous, crisp interlocking cells |
| **Hjaalmarch vs. Eastmarch** | Dissonant, reiterating adjacent pitches vs. bare fifths and speech-led epic narrative |
| **Eastmarch vs. Winterhold** | Articulated poetic *progression* vs. recurring wordless figures without strong cadence |
| **Haafingar vs. Whiterun vs. the Rift** | Polished galliard vs. elastic polska sway vs. bouncing jig |
| **Whiterun vs. Shared Nord Baseline** | Local triple dance lilt layered onto familiar strophic ballad language; not an unrelated genre |

## Generation Audition Checklist

For each render, record the prompt, model/version, seed if available, vocal mode, actual instrument family, and identity of the visible performer. Listen without seeing the assigned hold, then evaluate the following:

| Dimension | Questions |
|---|---|
| **Rhythmic Identity** | Can the proposed meter or pulse be heard? Does 5, 6/8, polska asymmetry, or triple galliard actually register when specified? |
| **Melodic Identity** | Are figures circular, descending, broad, speech-shaped, or interlocking as intended? |
| **Cadence and Silence** | Do phrases genuinely close, hang unresolved, or return without a final cadence as appropriate? |
| **Ornament and Delivery** | Is the voice syllabic, grace-note ornamented, wordless, or narrative in the specified manner? |
| **Lute Reduction** | Can the chosen idiom be expressed by one visible instrument, with or without the same performer's voice? |
| **Visible Source Count** | Any invisible chorus, backing drums, pads, or second instrumentalist? If so, do not mark as Phase 1 success. |
| **Nearest-Neighbor Separation** | In an unlabeled A/B comparison, could the render be mistaken for the closest other hold? |
| **Musical Quality** | Is it worth hearing again? Score this separately from fidelity: a beautiful wrong-style track is still wrong-style. |

Suggested annotation shorthand: **Pass / Partial / Fail** for each dimension; note the exact timestamp or phrase where success/failure is audible. Preserve unsuccessful auditions rather than treating better production polish as evidence of stylistic success.

## Open Decisions

1. **The Pale:** The *iorram*-inspired recipe is now the provisional default. Test whether its broad lines and recurring rowing pulse remain distinct from Whiterun and Winterhold. The original coastal-air material above is historical research.
2. **Reach comparison:** Puirt-à-beul is the provisional base; sean-nós is the sole admitted challenger. Test both against their nearest neighbors before choosing a replacement or admitting an alternate. Whiterun polska and other within-hold branches remain deferred.
3. **Single-bard constraints:** Hjaalmarch now tests instrumental sutartinės on one lute. The other bases use one singer accompanying themself on lute, with wordless Winterhold. Recorded ensembles do not establish that these reductions will work.
4. **Validation:** No rendered audio has been scored against this eleven-style baseline here. Every base must pass the spec's listening tests before additional variations are admitted.
